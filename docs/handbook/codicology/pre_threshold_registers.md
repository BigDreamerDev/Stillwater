# The Senion-Square Tradition

Notes on the codicological conventions of the Pre-Threshold registers

The Stillwater archive holds, in its lower stacks, a small but persistent group of manuscript registers conventionally assigned to the Pre-Threshold tradition. Their provenance remains disputed. Their dating has not stabilised. The hands responsible for them are unnamed.

Despite this uncertainty, the registers share a set of marginal practices so consistent that they are now treated as a distinct codicological system rather than as decoration, notation, or accidental scribal habit.

## The outer square

Each leaf bears, in its outer margin, a square chart of thirty-six cells.

The chart is arranged as six lines of six. Each cell contains a single glyph. No cell is empty. No cell is repeated within the square. The ordering of the glyphs appears fixed across the surviving Pre-Threshold material.

The steadiness of the hand used in the square is notable. In many registers it is more controlled than the body text, suggesting that the chart was not added as an afterthought. It was part of the page’s working structure.

Earlier cataloguers described the square variously as:

```txt
a concordance
a mnemonic table
a pronunciation aid
a foliation key
```

None of these explanations is sufficient. The square does not merely identify glyphs. It establishes position.

## The gutter figures

Opposite significant words in the body text, the scribes place strings of paired figures in the gutter margin.

Each figure is between one and six.

The pairs are written without punctuation. In several registers they are also written without spacing, producing bands of figures that can be mistaken for numerical references or damaged foliation marks.

Read alone, the figures offer little. Read against the outer square, they become positional instructions.

The first figure in a pair appears to identify one axis of the square. The second identifies the other. Taken together, each pair indicates a single cell.

The scribes do not explain this relationship directly. They appear to assume that any authorised reader would already understand the square’s use.

## The fivefold bands

The paired figures are not continuous across the page. They are arranged in bands of five.

This fivefold grouping is one of the most stable features of the tradition. It appears across registers, across scribal hands, and across damaged leaves where other features have become uncertain.

A band contains five positional pairs before the next band begins.

This suggests that the figures are not read as isolated references only. The order of the figures within each band matters. So does the boundary between one band and the next.

The Foundation’s current working position is that each band should be treated as a small unit of rearrangement before it is read back against the square.

Readers are cautioned not to assume that the first visible pair is necessarily the first recovered glyph.

## The tidal reading

In some later Pre-Threshold hands, the recovered line is not read straight across once restored.

Instead, the scribes appear to set the recovered signs into a narrow run of seven before taking them up again by descent and return. The line falls, rises, and falls again, like a tide crossing the same stones from alternating sides.

This produces a second ordering after the square has already been consulted.

The number seven is stable in the Codicology material, though no register gives a reason for it. Some archivists connect it to lower-stack routing conventions. Others treat it as an older scribal measure later adopted by Codicology.

The important point is that the restored signs are not always in their final order after the fivefold bands are resolved.

Where the marginal note resists reading, the line should be set in rows of seven and recovered by columns, alternating the direction of each column.

## Direction of reading

The body text provides the ordinary line of sight. The margins provide the recoverable line.

In practical terms, the reader must move between:

```txt
the outer square
the gutter pairings
the fivefold bands
the tidal reading
```

The square gives the available signs.  
The pairings give their positions.  
The fivefold bands govern their first ordering.  
The tidal reading governs their final recovery.

The result is not a translation in the usual sense. It is a restoration of a concealed reading line.

This is why Pre-Threshold notes often refer to “recovering” a word rather than “deciphering” it.

## On the absence of a key

No separate key has been found among the Pre-Threshold holdings.

This absence has sometimes been misread as evidence that the system is incomplete. It is more likely that the key was never external. The square itself provides the necessary fixed arrangement. The banding provides the method of handling the coordinates.

The difficulty lies not in discovering a password, but in recognising the convention.

The system is therefore considered no-key in the Foundation’s current classification.

## Provenance of the designation

The term Pre-Threshold is modern. It is not used by the scribes themselves.

The designation was adopted by the Foundation to distinguish these registers from post-audit material held elsewhere in the lower stacks. The boundary between the two bodies of material is defined by the audit.

The audit, in turn, is defined by reference to a date that has not yet occurred.

## Restricted access

Pre-Threshold registers are catalogued under Rank Codicology.

Direct consultation requires a reading-room key issued by the Office of Inherited Silences and is permitted for a single sitting only.

Notes taken during consultation are not to be removed from the reading room. Photographs are not permitted at any rank.

The outer square may be transcribed by hand.

The gutter pairings may not.
